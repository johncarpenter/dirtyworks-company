# Problem interview kit

**Purpose:** collect comparable evidence about recent knowledge-access events without teaching the prospect the answer.  
**Length:** 25–35 minutes  
**Use during:** Phase 1 validation

## Research discipline

- Interview behavior and recent events before opinions about AI.
- Ask for permission before recording; otherwise take structured notes.
- Do not request customer-confidential documents during an exploratory interview.
- Do not pitch until the event, consequence, current alternative, ownership, and urgency are understood.
- Preserve exact buyer language, but never put confidential or identifying details into cross-customer research notes without permission.
- Mark observations as evidence, inference, or interviewer interpretation.

## Opening

> I’m researching where established service companies lose time or consistency because a current operating answer is hard to find or lives with one experienced person. This is not a software demo. I’d like to understand the last real example in your business, what happened, and what you already do about it. I’ll keep company-specific details private and can anonymize the notes. Is that a useful way to spend 30 minutes?

Ask whether the participant consents to named notes, anonymized notes, and any recording. No recording is the default without explicit consent.

## Core sequence

### 1. Recent event

- Tell me about the last time someone could not find or verify the current answer, document, or procedure.
- When was it?
- What was the person trying to accomplish?
- Where did they look first, second, and third?
- Who did they eventually ask?
- How did they know the answer was current?

### 2. Consequence

- What waited or changed while they searched?
- Roughly how much elapsed time and hands-on time did it consume?
- Did it affect a customer, crew, project, estimate, invoice, schedule, quality, or risk?
- Was any work repeated or corrected?
- How often does something similar happen?
- Who absorbs the most interruptions?

Do not force a dollar estimate. Time, delay, rework, risk, and frustration are separate forms of evidence.

### 3. Pattern and scope

- Which question types repeat?
- Which roles ask and answer them?
- What questions dominate a new employee’s first 90 days?
- What cannot proceed when one specific person is away?
- Which one question domain would be valuable to fix first?
- What should never be answered by a system without a person checking?

### 4. Information and systems

- Where does the useful information usually live?
- Which source is considered authoritative when versions conflict?
- Who owns updates?
- How much of the important answer exists only in people’s heads or messages?
- Are access permissions already reliable?
- What tools have you tried: search, SharePoint/Drive, knowledge base, Copilot/Gemini/ChatGPT, an MSP, or process documentation?
- What worked and failed?

### 5. Buying and urgency

- Who owns the business consequence?
- Who would own the source material and access decisions?
- What would make this important in the next 90 days?
- What else would compete for the same budget or attention?
- Have you paid for related work before? What form did it take?
- What proof would be needed before committing to implementation?

### 6. Concept and price test

Only after the evidence sequence:

> I’m testing a managed service that starts with a paid reliability review. It maps one question domain, approved sources, permissions, baseline, risks, and real evaluation questions. If it is feasible, a bounded launch gives one role group sourced answers, followed by ongoing quality, source, access, support, and improvement operations. What part fits what you described, and what does not?

Then test price without apologizing:

> The current review hypothesis is about $2,500, and the managed service after a launch is expected to start around $2,500 per month, with licences separate. How would a decision at those levels be evaluated here?

Follow with:

- Is the issue priority, trust, scope, price, timing, or something else?
- What would need to be true for the review to be worth paying for?
- Who else should evaluate it?

Do not convert a refusal into a discount negotiation. Capture the mechanism behind the refusal.

## Closing

- Summarize the event and problem in the participant’s language.
- Ask what was misunderstood.
- If qualified, agree to a next conversation with the economic buyer and knowledge owner.
- If not qualified, ask permission for a later research follow-up.
- Ask for one introduction only when the participant clearly recognizes the pattern.

## Weekly synthesis

After each five interviews, aggregate without allowing one vivid story to dominate:

- repeated events and question domains;
- buyer and affected-role patterns;
- consequences with strongest evidence;
- current tools and workarounds;
- recurring objections by mechanism;
- source-rich versus source-poor distribution;
- price response by segment;
- disqualifiers and risk patterns;
- introductions and next commitments;
- hypotheses strengthened, weakened, or unchanged.

Do not change the ICP or positioning after every interview. Change when a repeatable pattern is supported or a critical assumption is disproven.
