# Dirtyworks.ai master business strategy

**Version:** 0.2  
**Date:** 2026-08-25  
**Status:** Working strategy with sponsor-approved market, brand, capacity, and budget constraints  
**Planning horizon:** First 18 months

## Executive decision

Dirtyworks.ai will build a **managed knowledge operations business**, enabled by AI, for established Alberta SMBs. The initial market lanes are professional-services firms—particularly accounting and HR/advisory businesses—and Calgary-area energy-service companies. Internally this is an AI MSP model. In the market it will lead with an operating result:

> Your team gets reliable answers from approved company information, and the system stays accurate, secure, and useful as the business changes.

The initial wedge is one economically important question domain for one role group—not a company-wide AI transformation. Dirtyworks.ai will configure and operate customer-owned technology, improve the source material behind it, measure answer reliability and use, and convert proven repeated work into separately governed workflows.

Dirtyworks.ai is not initially a software company, generic AI consultancy, traditional IT help desk, document migration firm, or unlimited automation shop. The business earns recurring revenue by taking responsibility for the operating layer that product vendors do not provide to smaller firms.

## Why this strategy can work

The mechanism has four parts:

1. Companies already have valuable operational knowledge, but it is fragmented, stale, inconsistently permissioned, or held by a few people.
2. Native AI products make conversational retrieval cheaper, which lowers implementation friction but commoditizes the interface.
3. Reliable business use still requires source ownership, access design, evaluations, adoption, incident handling, and continuous improvement.
4. Real usage produces evidence about missing knowledge and repeated work, creating qualified expansion opportunities.

The moat is therefore not a chat box or retrieval pipeline. It is a trusted operating relationship, vertical delivery knowledge, repeatable controls, measured results, and a library of generalized question patterns, evaluations, source practices, and workflow methods.

## Strategic choices

These are deliberate working choices. The alternatives remain real, but pursuing all of them at once would prevent learning.

| Dimension | Choice for initial validation | Why | Revisit trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Category | Managed knowledge operations; use “AI MSP” as an explanatory label | Leads with a recognizable result instead of a technology category | Buyers consistently self-identify as seeking an AI MSP |
| Market lanes | Alberta professional-services SMBs and Calgary-area energy-service SMBs | Sponsor direction; knowledge-dense work; accessible regional network; different risk/value patterns worth comparing | One lane fails the recognition/payment gate or a narrower segment clearly dominates |
| Customer size | 20–150 employees, normally $3M–$75M revenue | Enough shared complexity and budget without enterprise procurement | Delivery or sales data shows a tighter profitable band |
| Land scope | One role group, one answer domain, 1–3 approved source systems | Makes value and risk testable | Two launches prove a broader scope is equally reliable |
| Technology | Configure a customer-owned incumbent platform first | Portability, speed, trust, and no premature platform build | The same material platform gap appears in 3 customers |
| Sales motion | Half-time founder, warm introductions, one concentrated direct cohort, and one live MSP-partner experiment | Preserves comparable direct evidence while testing an available distribution advantage | Capacity or evidence makes a lane nonviable |
| Channel | Test referral, co-managed/co-branded, and white-label structures with the live MSP; prefer transparent co-managed delivery for the first pilot | White-label changes margin, learning, responsibility, and customer access; the lightest workable structure preserves evidence | Partner requirements or pilot evidence support another structure |
| Risk posture | Non-authoritative internal operating knowledge first | Limits privacy, safety, employment, and decision risk | Controls and insurance support a higher-risk use case |
| Economics | Knowledge service must be profitable on its own | Prevents subsidizing a commodity wedge indefinitely | Evidence shows expansion LTV reliably covers acquisition subsidy |
| Commercial model | Paid assessment, paid launch, recurring managed service; customer pays licences | Preserves scope, cash flow, and cost transparency | Buyers reject the structure for a consistent reason |
| Build posture | Service first; automate Dirty Works’ repeated work; productize last | Lets actual delivery reveal requirements | A component is repeated, stable, and margin-limiting |
| Brand | Dirtyworks.ai; working line “We do the work behind AI that works” | Sponsor-approved name/domain with a message tied to operational value | Brand research reveals a material trust or comprehension problem |
| Capacity/capital | Half-time founder; $5,000–$50,000 deployed by evidence gates | Sponsor-defined experiment constraints | Capital need, founder availability, or traction changes |

## Ideal customer profile

### Required conditions

- 20–150 employees with at least five people needing overlapping operating knowledge.
- A named executive sponsor and a named customer knowledge owner.
- Useful information already exists in approved sources.
- A repeated question domain affects response time, onboarding, estimating, project delivery, service, billing, or continuity.
- The customer will permit baseline measurement and user feedback.
- The source and user scope can be bounded.

### Strong signals

- Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace is already central to the business.
- More than one team, office, client-service group, project group, or office/field boundary.
- Growth, acquisition, new system implementation, new leadership, or impending retirement.
- Senior employees are repeatedly interrupted for routine answers.
- Different versions of procedures, templates, specifications, or commercial rules circulate.
- Staff already use public AI tools without an approved operating model.

### Disqualifiers for the initial program

- No accountable source owner.
- The important knowledge is almost entirely undocumented and the buyer will not fund capture.
- The buyer wants an autonomous decision maker, public chatbot, or guarantee of perfect answers.
- The initial use depends on health information, legal conclusions, employment decisions, safety-critical instructions, or regulated professional judgment.
- The customer will not permit least-privilege access testing or basic measurement.
- The expected service fee cannot plausibly be justified by the selected problem.

### Professional-services overlay

Good first domains include approved firm methods, engagement administration, service-delivery playbooks, templates, software procedures, onboarding, and internal operations. Accounting and HR/advisory firms contain attractive knowledge density but also client financial information, employee/candidate data, legal interpretation, and regulated professional judgment.

The first scope should exclude client records, employee/candidate decisions, tax/accounting conclusions, legal advice, and automated professional judgment. The purpose is to prove internal operating knowledge before expanding the risk boundary.

### Energy-services overlay

Target service, engineering, maintenance, technology, environmental, and project/vendor businesses serving the energy sector—not major producers with enterprise procurement as the default beachhead. Good first domains include commercial/project administration, approved proposal material, client onboarding requirements, internal systems procedures, project closeout, and non-authoritative lessons learned.

The first scope should exclude safety-critical operating instructions, control-system actions, engineering conclusions, environmental/regulatory determinations, and unsupervised field decisions. Energy adjacency may improve willingness-to-pay, but consequence, security, and procurement cost must remain compatible with an SMB service price.

## Buyer and job to be done

The economic buyer is normally the owner, managing partner, COO, general manager, or operations leader. The internal champion is often a practice/operations manager, office manager, project administrator, finance lead, service coordinator, or long-tenured employee who absorbs repeated questions.

The primary job is:

> When staff need a current operating answer, help them find and verify it without waiting for the one person who knows where it lives.

Supporting jobs include shortening role onboarding, protecting continuity, standardizing answers, revealing missing documentation, governing workplace AI use, and identifying stable workflow improvements.

## Offer architecture

The commercial ladder is intentionally progressive:

1. **Knowledge Reliability Review** — determine whether the problem and source base justify implementation.
2. **Managed Knowledge Launch** — implement one bounded answer domain and prove reliability and use.
3. **Knowledge Operations** — run the service, sources, permissions, evaluations, adoption, incidents, reporting, and improvements.
4. **Managed Workflow Expansion** — repair, assist, integrate, or automate a proven repeated job under a separate scope.

Each step can produce value without forcing the next. Expansion is earned through evidence, not assumed in the contract.

## Market position

### Positioning statement

For established Alberta SMBs whose staff lose time finding and verifying operating information, Dirtyworks.ai is the managed knowledge operations partner that makes approved company information reliably usable and keeps it that way. Unlike software licences, generic AI consulting, or traditional IT support, Dirtyworks.ai owns the ongoing quality, governance, adoption, and improvement work across the customer’s chosen systems.

### Message hierarchy

1. **Outcome:** reliable, verifiable company answers.
2. **Business effect:** fewer interruptions, faster onboarding and response, lower key-person dependency.
3. **Managed promise:** sources, permissions, quality, support, and improvement stay operated.
4. **Method:** customer-owned AI and knowledge tools, configured around real work.
5. **Expansion:** repeated, stable work can become assisted or automated workflows.

Avoid leading with RAG, agents, vector databases, embeddings, tokens, or “AI transformation.” Those terms describe implementation choices, not the buyer’s problem.

## Competitive strategy

Dirtyworks.ai competes with four alternatives:

| Alternative | Strength | Weakness Dirty Works can exploit | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do nothing / ask the expert | Familiar and apparently free | Interruptions, delays, key-person risk, inconsistent answers | Quantify one recent costly pattern |
| Native Microsoft, Google, or OpenAI capability | Low licence cost, integrated, improving quickly | Does not create source ownership, evaluation discipline, rollout, or an operating cadence by itself | Configure and manage it; never claim the licence is insufficient in all cases |
| Traditional MSP or implementer | Existing trust and technical access | Often optimized for infrastructure or a one-time deployment | Co-manage with explicit boundaries or compete on knowledge operations |
| Custom AI consultancy | Flexible and technically deep | Project ends; bespoke work can be expensive and difficult to operate | Sell a bounded launch followed by accountable recurring operations |

The most important competitor is inaction. The sales process must establish economic pain before demonstrating technology.

## Delivery advantage

Dirtyworks.ai will standardize the invisible operating system around each implementation:

- source inventory, ownership, classification, and freshness;
- least-privilege access and permission regression tests;
- a customer-approved evaluation set based on real questions;
- abstention, contradiction, and escalation paths;
- user rollout and adoption routines;
- service incidents, changes, reporting, and offboarding;
- a gap-to-workflow classification method;
- platform-portable customer records.

These assets belong in documentation and internal tools. Customer confidential content will not be pooled or used to create cross-customer advantage without explicit lawful authorization.

## Economic model

Revenue comes from paid diagnosis, implementation, recurring management, and separately scoped expansion. Licences and model use are passed through or bought by the customer. Raw consumption markup is not a strategic margin source.

Initial economic targets:

| Metric | Target | Why it matters |
|---|---:|---|
| Paid assessment gross margin | 60%+ | Proves discovery is a product, not free presales |
| Launch gross margin after second deployment | 50%+ | Leaves room for rework while the playbook matures |
| Recurring service gross margin after 90 days | 60%+ | Supports a durable managed-service model |
| Recurring revenue coverage of fixed operating cost | By 10 core-equivalent accounts | Creates a practical founder-led base |
| Revenue concentration | No customer above 25% by account 10 | Limits dependency |
| Expansion | At least 30% of qualified accounts within 12 months | Tests the land-and-expand thesis without requiring it for survival |

Margins use fully loaded delivery cost, platform cost, contractors, and unrecoverable support time. Founder time is not free.

## 18-month outcomes

### Base case

- 20 validated buyer interviews.
- 5 paid Knowledge Reliability Reviews.
- 3 Managed Knowledge Launches.
- 5 managed-service customers and 2 referenceable case studies.
- $20,000–$30,000 monthly recurring revenue.
- 60% steady-state recurring gross margin.
- One repeatable vertical playbook and one co-managed MSP offer ready for testing.
- One paid MSP-sourced design pilot with channel economics and responsibility evidence.

### Upside case

- 10 managed-service customers.
- $45,000+ monthly recurring revenue.
- A delivery lead or knowledge operations manager hired from recurring margin.
- Two repeatable managed workflows attached to the core service.

### Stop or redesign conditions

- Buyers describe the problem but will not pay for diagnosis.
- Knowledge launches do not produce repeated use or an improved operating measure.
- Recurring labour remains proportional to document volume or customer exceptions and cannot reach 50% gross margin.
- Native platforms solve the selected job well enough that customers do not value ongoing operations.
- Privacy, security, insurance, or contractual requirements exceed what the target price can support.

Stopping a specific offer is not the same as ending the company. Evidence may support a different vertical, workflow-first offer, channel model, or operational advisory service.
