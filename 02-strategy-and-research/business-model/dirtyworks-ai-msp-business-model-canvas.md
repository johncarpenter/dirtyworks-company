# Dirty Works — AI MSP Business Model Canvas

**Working version:** August 2026  
**Primary hypothesis:** Alberta-first, managed AI and knowledge operations for established service businesses  
**Land motion:** managed company knowledge  
**Expansion motion:** knowledge gaps → workflow improvement → managed automation

> **Supersession note — 2026-08-25:** This canvas preserves the initial construction/field-service hypothesis. Sponsor direction subsequently set professional-services SMBs and Calgary-area energy-service SMBs as the active market lanes and elevated an available MSP channel opportunity. The [master business strategy](../company-strategy/business-strategy.md) and [MSP channel strategy](../channels/msp-channel-strategy.md) are authoritative where they differ from this canvas.

## Executive thesis

Dirty Works would operate as an **AI Managed Service Provider**: the outside team responsible for selecting, configuring, connecting, governing, supporting, and improving a customer's AI systems.

The company would not begin by asking customers to identify something they want automated. Most businesses tolerate familiar inefficiency because the current method still works and the people involved know how to perform it. They also rarely describe the resulting condition as a “knowledge problem.”

Dirty Works would instead land with a concrete capability:

> **Give your team reliable answers from the company information you already have.**

The first implementation would likely be a private company-knowledge product—an LLM wiki, enterprise search layer, or retrieval-augmented generation system—that connects approved sources and returns answers with citations and permissions.

That product has two jobs:

1. Deliver immediate value by reducing the time spent searching, asking, re-explaining, and recreating information.
2. Reveal what the business should improve next by observing repeated questions, missing answers, weak source material, manual handoffs, and recurring requests.

The durable service is therefore not “RAG hosting.” It is the ongoing operation of the customer's knowledge, data, AI tools, and selected workflows.

## The strategic mechanism

```text
Scattered company information
            ↓
Approved, permission-aware knowledge layer
            ↓
Reliable answers with sources inside daily work
            ↓
Evidence: repeated questions, gaps, delays and handoffs
            ↓
Knowledge cleanup, data integration and workflow automation
            ↓
Ongoing monitoring, support, governance and improvement
            ↺
```

This is a land-and-expand loop:

- **Land:** make existing knowledge easier to use.
- **Learn:** measure what employees ask, where answers fail, and which requests recur.
- **Improve:** repair missing or contradictory source material.
- **Automate:** turn stable, frequent requests into managed workflows.
- **Operate:** monitor usage, quality, permissions, exceptions, vendors, and business value.
- **Expand:** connect more sources, departments, and workflows.

The knowledge layer should generate qualified evidence for automation. It should not be treated as proof that every repeated question ought to become an agent or workflow.

## The problem customers can recognize

“You have a knowledge problem” is unlikely to create urgency. The observable symptoms are more sellable:

- Employees repeatedly interrupt the same experienced people.
- Staff cannot find the current procedure, specification, price, policy, or project history.
- New hires take too long to become useful.
- Different employees give different answers to the same question.
- Past work is recreated because nobody can locate it.
- Important operating knowledge lives in inboxes, personal folders, and people's memories.
- Customers wait while employees search across systems.
- The company becomes exposed when a long-tenured employee is absent or leaves.
- Employees use public AI tools independently because no approved company capability exists.

The customer-facing promise should therefore be about **answer access, continuity, consistency, and ownership**, not knowledge architecture or retrieval technology.

## The qualification split

The knowledge-first motion creates two materially different customer types.

### Knowledge-rich but inaccessible

The company already has useful documents, records, policies, project files, messages, and system data, but employees cannot find or combine them easily.

This is the best fit for a fast first implementation. Dirty Works can connect a bounded set of trusted sources and demonstrate value quickly.

### Knowledge-poor and person-dependent

The company relies heavily on oral practice, personal judgment, informal messages, and undocumented exceptions.

This customer may have greater underlying pain, but an LLM wiki cannot retrieve knowledge that has never been recorded. Dirty Works must first capture, reconcile, and approve the knowledge. That is a service-heavy engagement with different pricing, timing, and risk.

The sales process should determine which condition exists before promising a rapid launch.

## Business Model Canvas

### 1. Customer segments

**Primary starting hypothesis**

- Alberta service businesses with roughly 10–75 employees and enough operating complexity to create repeated questions and cross-system handoffs.
- Initial vertical test: construction, field service, and project-based contractors with office, field, estimating, finance, and project coordination roles.
- Buyers: owner, general manager, COO, operations manager, or another leader accountable for administrative capacity and continuity.
- Internal champions: operations coordinators, project administrators, service managers, finance leaders, and senior employees who currently answer everyone else's questions.

**Strong knowledge-first characteristics**

- Information is spread across Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, shared drives, email, CRM, accounting, project systems, or vertical software.
- Five or more people need access to overlapping operating knowledge.
- The company has useful source material but poor retrieval, inconsistent organization, or key-person dependence.
- Repeated questions or document searches affect customer response, onboarding, project delivery, billing, or compliance.
- A leader will own source approval, access decisions, and adoption.

**Poor early fits**

- Solopreneurs and very small firms with little shared knowledge or budget.
- Companies seeking an unsupervised public chatbot rather than an operating relationship.
- Firms with no accountable source owner or willingness to clean contradictory information.
- Highly sensitive or regulated use cases before Dirty Works has the required security, privacy, insurance, and contractual capability.
- Companies expecting the system to infer undocumented practices automatically.

### 2. Value propositions

**Immediate value**

- Employees can ask company-specific questions and receive grounded answers with visible sources.
- Less time is spent searching, interrupting experienced staff, and recreating previous work.
- New and occasional employees can reach approved information faster.
- The company reduces dependence on a few people who hold essential operating knowledge.

**Managed-service value**

- Dirty Works takes responsibility for the AI environment rather than handing over another tool.
- Sources, permissions, apps, models, costs, and user access are actively administered.
- Answer quality, unanswered questions, failed retrievals, feedback, and vendor changes are monitored.
- Employees receive training and support in the context of their actual work.
- Leaders receive a recurring report on use, gaps, risks, and realized value.

**Expansion value**

- Usage reveals which questions and handoffs are frequent enough to improve or automate.
- Missing knowledge becomes a visible operating backlog instead of remaining hidden in interruptions and workarounds.
- Proven workflows can be connected to business systems with human approvals and exception handling.
- The customer gains a continuing AI and automation capability without staffing an internal department.

### 3. Channels

- Founder-led conversations with owners and operations leaders in a narrowly defined initial vertical.
- Warm introductions from prior automation work and professional relationships.
- Traditional MSPs that manage infrastructure and user systems but do not want to build a knowledge and workflow practice.
- Fractional COOs and CFOs, bookkeepers, software implementers, cybersecurity advisers, and privacy professionals.
- Construction and field-service associations, peer groups, and operator roundtables.
- A bounded “company answer test” using a prospect's approved documents to demonstrate the experience without exposing production data.
- Case studies expressed in operating terms: answer time, interruptions avoided, onboarding time, unresolved-question rate, and workflows discovered.

Traditional MSPs can be either a channel or a competitor. Dirty Works must decide whether the preferred motion is direct, referral-based, co-managed, or white-labelled before building channel-specific systems.

### 4. Customer relationships

- A paid readiness and knowledge-mapping engagement before implementation.
- A named Dirty Works AI operations lead and a named customer knowledge owner.
- Structured onboarding for source selection, permissions, answer evaluation, and user rollout.
- Weekly reviews during launch; monthly value and operations reviews after stabilization.
- A clear support path for wrong answers, missing knowledge, access problems, and workflow incidents.
- Quarterly roadmap sessions to decide which gaps to document, integrate, automate, or deliberately leave manual.
- Explicit service levels, change boundaries, approval responsibilities, and offboarding/portability terms.

The relationship resembles an MSP, but “unlimited AI and automation” should not be offered. The agreement must distinguish routine operation from new implementation work.

### 5. Revenue streams

The following are test structures, not fixed pricing decisions.

**Knowledge Readiness and Map**

- One-time paid assessment covering sources, permissions, knowledge owners, use cases, risks, and a first-launch plan.
- Preserve the previous plan's approximate diagnostic range of **$1,500–$3,000** until sales evidence supports changing it.

**Managed Knowledge Launch**

- One-time implementation for a bounded group of sources, users, and question types.
- Includes source connection, permission design, answer evaluations, rollout, training, and an initial value baseline.
- Preserve the previous pilot hypothesis of approximately **$6,000–$12,000**, while recognizing that undocumented or highly fragmented knowledge may require a larger capture project.

**AI MSP recurring service**

- Monthly fee for workspace administration, knowledge operations, monitoring, support, training, governance, vendor management, reporting, and an agreed improvement allowance.
- Preserve the previous managed-service hypothesis of approximately **$1,500–$4,000 per month** as an initial test range.
- Price should vary by users, sources, workflows, data sensitivity, support window, service level, and change volume—not simply token consumption.

**Expansion work**

- Additional source systems, departments, agents, integrations, and managed workflows.
- Larger workflow builds remain separately scoped unless the recurring package includes explicit implementation capacity.

**Third-party and usage costs**

- Customer-owned licences and platform/API costs can be passed through transparently or included up to a stated allowance.
- A procurement or administration fee can be explicit, but raw model-usage markup should not become the main source of margin.

**Three commercial structures worth testing**

1. Client-owned technology plus a Dirty Works management retainer: strongest portability and cost transparency.
2. Bundled managed service with an included allowance: simplest customer invoice but greater margin and usage risk.
3. Department or workflow subscription: clearest connection to an operating outcome but narrower scope.

### 6. Key activities

- Knowledge and data-source inventory.
- Permission mapping, security review, and data classification.
- Source cleanup, deduplication, ownership, freshness, and approval rules.
- Configuration or development of the knowledge product and its connectors.
- Retrieval and answer evaluations using real, approved questions.
- Citation, access-control, and sensitive-information testing.
- User onboarding, training, adoption support, and office hours.
- Monitoring usage, answer quality, unanswered questions, feedback, latency, costs, and incidents.
- Maintaining a knowledge-gap backlog with customer owners.
- Converting suitable repeated requests into workflows with defined human and automation boundaries.
- Monthly value reporting and quarterly expansion planning.
- Vendor, model, API, and app-change maintenance.

### 7. Key resources

- A multi-customer knowledge product or repeatable reference architecture with strong tenant isolation.
- Connectors for common document, email, CRM, accounting, and vertical systems.
- Permission-aware retrieval and customer-controlled source access.
- Evaluation sets, regression tests, audit logs, monitoring, and feedback tools.
- Knowledge-readiness, source-approval, and knowledge-capture playbooks.
- Reusable workflow patterns and integration components.
- The founder's automation experience, prior-project network, and ability to translate operating work into systems.
- Security, privacy, service, incident-response, and offboarding templates.
- Over time, vertical question patterns, knowledge taxonomies, benchmarks, and workflow evidence.

### 8. Key partners

- AI model, cloud, search, retrieval, and hosting providers.
- Business AI workspace and productivity-suite providers.
- Traditional MSPs and managed security providers.
- Vertical SaaS, accounting, CRM, field-service, and project-platform vendors.
- Privacy, cybersecurity, legal, and insurance specialists.
- Fractional operations and finance leaders who can identify economic value and sponsor adoption.
- Industry associations and trusted local advisers.

Production accounts, workspaces, and business data should normally remain customer-owned. Dirty Works should operate through delegated access unless an explicit reseller or hosting arrangement provides suitable customer rights, security obligations, portability, and termination terms.

### 9. Cost structure

- Discovery, implementation, data cleanup, and integration labour.
- Ongoing support, monitoring, evaluation, reporting, and improvement labour.
- AI model, search, storage, hosting, vector/database, observability, and automation-platform costs.
- Product licensing or development and connector maintenance.
- Security controls, audits, privacy work, insurance, and legal contracts.
- Sales, demonstrations, training content, travel, and channel commissions.
- Non-billable vendor changes, incidents, and customer-specific exceptions.
- Product and service management needed to prevent every account from becoming a unique architecture.

## The first offer

### Working name: Dirty Works Company Knowledge

**Promise**

> Your team can ask questions about the business and get reliable answers from approved company sources—with the references needed to verify them.

**Bounded initial scope**

- One business unit or role group.
- One to three approved source systems.
- A defined group of question types.
- Customer-approved users and permission model.
- Answers with source references and an “I don't know” path.
- A feedback and escalation mechanism.
- Initial evaluation set created from real employee questions.
- Launch training and a 30-day value review.

**What is explicitly excluded unless separately scoped**

- Migrating or cleaning the customer's entire information estate.
- Making authoritative legal, safety, clinical, financial, or employment decisions.
- Automatically acting on business systems without defined approvals.
- Recovering undocumented knowledge without interviews and customer validation.
- Guaranteeing that every answer is correct or that every source is current.
- Unlimited new integrations, agents, or workflow builds.

## The value scorecard

The first implementation needs operating measures rather than a generic AI-adoption score.

**Leading indicators**

- Percentage of invited users who ask work-related questions repeatedly.
- Number and type of questions asked.
- Percentage of answers with usable approved sources.
- User feedback and escalation rate.
- Unanswered, low-confidence, or contradictory questions.
- Source freshness and ownership coverage.

**Outcome indicators**

- Time required to find or verify an answer.
- Interruptions handled by designated subject-matter experts.
- Time for a new employee to perform selected tasks independently.
- Rework caused by outdated or inconsistent information.
- Customer or internal response time for selected question types.
- Number and estimated value of workflow opportunities revealed.

Logins, tokens, and total questions are not sufficient evidence of business value on their own.

## Sales and discovery mechanism

Do not ask, “Do you have a knowledge problem?” Ask for recent operating events:

- Tell me about the last time someone could not find the current answer or document.
- Who gets interrupted when another employee does not know what to do?
- What questions do new employees ask repeatedly during their first three months?
- What stops when one particular employee is unavailable?
- Where do people look before they finally ask someone?
- When did two people last follow different versions of the same process?
- Which customer questions require searching through several systems?
- What information is recreated because locating the previous work takes too long?

The qualification goal is to find a repeated, economically relevant question domain—not merely a large pile of documents.

## Expansion logic

Not every observed pattern should be automated. Use the following sequence:

1. **Answer:** Can approved existing information resolve the request?
2. **Repair:** Is the real problem missing, stale, contradictory, or ownerless knowledge?
3. **Structure:** Does information need to move from documents into a system of record?
4. **Assist:** Can AI prepare a draft, summary, classification, or recommendation for review?
5. **Automate:** Are the inputs, rules, exceptions, approvals, and economic value stable enough for action?
6. **Operate:** Can Dirty Works monitor the workflow and support it within a defined service level?

This prevents the knowledge product from becoming an indiscriminate agent-sales engine.

## Defensibility

RAG and company-search features will continue to become cheaper and appear inside existing software. The defensible asset cannot be the chat interface.

Dirty Works can accumulate advantage through:

- Trusted access and an ongoing operating relationship.
- Vertical understanding of where useful knowledge lives and how it is applied.
- Permission, evaluation, and governance practices that make answers dependable.
- A growing library of real question patterns, failure modes, and workflow candidates.
- Reusable connectors, source mappings, approval patterns, and evaluations.
- Demonstrated before/after operational results.
- The ability to progress from answers to data repair and cross-system workflow ownership.
- Distribution through MSPs and other advisers that already hold customer trust.

Customer information should not be pooled or reused across accounts without explicit, lawful permission. Defensibility should come from generalized methods and patterns, not possession of customer-confidential data.

## Principal risks and countermeasures

| Risk | Mechanism | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| The customer does not recognize the problem | “Knowledge management” sounds abstract or optional | Sell recognizable symptoms and one bounded answer domain |
| Empty-corpus problem | Important knowledge has never been documented | Qualify knowledge-rich and knowledge-poor firms differently; price capture separately |
| Bad-source problem | The system faithfully retrieves outdated or contradictory information | Establish owners, approval state, freshness rules, citations, and conflict escalation |
| Permission leakage | Retrieval exposes information to the wrong employee | Preserve source permissions, use least privilege, and test access boundaries |
| Confident wrong answers | Plausible output damages trust or causes action | Cite sources, permit abstention, test with real questions, and keep high-risk judgment human |
| Weak adoption | Employees keep asking familiar people or using public tools | Design around real questions, involve subject-matter experts, train users, and support the rollout |
| Commodity product | Existing suites add acceptable company search and AI | Own cross-system operations, quality, governance, and workflow improvement—not the interface |
| Scope explosion | “Manage our AI” becomes unlimited IT, data cleanup, training, and development | Define managed assets, support classes, improvement allowance, and change rules |
| Support burden | Source and vendor changes create invisible labour | Monitor changes, standardize connectors, price complexity, and maintain service limits |
| Surveillance concern | Question logs feel like employee monitoring | Collect only necessary telemetry, be transparent, aggregate reporting, and define retention/access |
| No automation expansion | The knowledge tool is useful but does not reveal valuable workflows | Treat conversion as a hypothesis; preserve a viable standalone recurring knowledge service |
| Channel conflict | Traditional MSPs see Dirty Works as entering their account | Define co-management boundaries and commercial terms before channel outreach |

## What changes from the previous Dirty Works canvas

| Previous emphasis | AI MSP rebuild |
|---|---|
| Start with a buyer-recognized repetitive workflow | Start with reliable access to an important body of company knowledge |
| Diagnose automation candidates through interviews and process mapping | Add real usage and unanswered questions as a continuing discovery signal |
| Manage one automation after launch | Manage the AI environment, knowledge layer, and selected workflows |
| Automation is the land offer | Knowledge access is the land offer; automation is an evidence-led expansion |
| Primary recurring value is workflow uptime and improvement | Recurring value includes knowledge quality, permissions, adoption, support, governance, and workflow operation |
| MSPs are mainly partners | MSPs become a potentially important referral, co-managed, or white-label channel |

The previous principle still holds: Dirty Works should sell an operating result rather than a collection of tools. The AI MSP framing changes the breadth of the relationship and the order in which value is created; it does not justify unbounded service.

## Strategic decisions still open

1. **Vertical or horizontal:** begin with construction/field service, or sell the knowledge-first offer across several knowledge-dense service industries.
2. **Product strategy:** configure an existing LLM wiki/RAG product, develop a managed reference architecture, or eventually build proprietary software.
3. **Channel strategy:** sell directly, partner with MSPs, or offer a white-label capability.
4. **Service boundary:** manage only the knowledge and AI layer, or also take responsibility for data cleanup and selected business workflows.
5. **Commercial packaging:** customer-owned stack plus retainer, bundled managed service, or department/workflow subscription.
6. **Risk posture:** begin with non-sensitive operating knowledge, or invest early in regulated and confidential use cases.
7. **Standalone viability:** require the knowledge service to be profitable by itself, or treat it as a deliberately subsidized acquisition path to automation work.

## Validation plan

### Phase 1 — Recognition test

- Interview owners, operations leaders, new employees, and the people who answer repeated questions.
- Ask about the last real search, interruption, inconsistent answer, onboarding delay, or key-person absence.
- Test symptom-based language against “AI,” “knowledge management,” and “automation” language.
- Determine whether a leader will pay to fix one answer domain before seeing a broader automation roadmap.

**Gate:** at least five buyers describe a materially similar symptom without being taught the category, and at least three will expose approved sources for a paid readiness engagement.

### Phase 2 — First-value test

- Launch a bounded knowledge implementation for one question domain.
- Establish baseline answer time, interruption volume, or another relevant operating measure.
- Test citations, permissions, abstention, feedback, and source freshness.
- Measure whether employees return without founder prompting.

**Gate:** users repeatedly obtain useful, verifiable answers and one agreed operating measure improves meaningfully.

### Phase 3 — Expansion test

- Review unanswered and repeated questions with the customer.
- Classify each as an answer, source repair, structured-data, assisted-work, or automation opportunity.
- Price the next intervention rather than giving it away as support.
- Track whether the knowledge launch converts into a sustainable managed agreement and qualified expansion.

**Gate:** the first accounts demonstrate either profitable standalone recurring knowledge operations or repeatable expansion into higher-value managed workflows.
