**Composition reference only:** [AI scores on his own team](../assets/photography/campaign-concepts/ai-in-the-80s-human-player-own-goal-v1.png)—use its comic staging, uniform, and nameplate; its visible side profile is superseded and not production-compliant  
**Concept record:** [Campaign concepts v2](../assets/photography/campaign-concepts/campaign-concepts-v2.prompt.md)

> **Character decision — 2026-08-26:** `THE COACH` is now a gruff older man. The earlier female-coach keyframes remain useful as composition references but no longer satisfy the character bible and must be regenerated before production use.

> **AI visibility decision — 2026-08-26:** `AI` has a human body but is not presented as an identifiable human character. His face is never shown—not frontally, in three-quarter view, as a readable profile, in a reflection, or in an in-world photograph. Frame him from the back or rear three-quarter angle; a side-body view is permitted only when his head is turned away or the face is fully excluded by the helmet-and-head angle, crop, or composition. This is both a meaning rule and a continuity control. The earlier own-goal concept frame is now composition-only because it reveals his profile.

## Purpose

Test whether a recurring fictional sports story can make the managed-operations argument memorable:

> AI can be exceptionally capable and still fail when nobody supplies context, boundaries, coaching, support, and responsibility for the whole system.

The campaign is pro-capability and pro-operation. `AI` is not stupid, malicious, robotic, or a substitute for the team. He is represented by a gifted human-bodied player whose narrow execution can be brilliant while his situational judgment is incomplete. The body gives the team someone to play with; the hidden face keeps him an embodied capability rather than a literal person.

This is initially a social campaign. Do not revise the main website photography system around it unless the campaign creates sustained recognition with the intended business audience—not only reach among hockey or nostalgia audiences.

## The premise

A badly run fictional minor-league hockey team signs a mysterious new player named `AI`. He appears as a human-bodied athlete, but the audience never sees his face. He is immediately the most talented player anyone in the league has seen. He skates faster, remembers every play, reads every statistic, and executes with impossible precision.

He also:

- scores on the wrong net;
- follows the wrong version of the playbook;
- optimizes his own shift while breaking the line change;
- gives the technically correct pass to a teammate who is no longer on the ice;
- memorizes every opponent except the one the team is playing;
- solves the visible task while missing the operating context.

The joke is never that AI has no value. The joke is that the team thought talent would replace coaching and operations.

## Strategic message

### Audience takeaway

> The tool may be excellent. Somebody still has to run the team.

### Connection to Dirtyworks.ai

The team is a compact metaphor for a company AI portfolio:

| Hockey world | Managed AI world |
|---|---|
| Star player | Capable AI product |
| Coach | Operating model and accountable owner |
| Playbook | Approved process, source, and policy |
| Line combinations | Users, roles, and workflows |
| Game situation | Company context and current conditions |
| Practice | Training, evaluation, and supported use |
| Equipment manager | Accounts, access, licences, and recovery |
| Scoreboard | Cost, quality, adoption, and service signals |
| Video review | Evidence, incident review, and improvement |

Do not explain this entire mapping inside an ad. Each episode should dramatize one contradiction, then let the end card make one operating point.

## Tone

- Deadpan rather than broad slapstick.
- Affectionate toward the players and people doing the work.
- Scrappy, rueful, and observant.
- AI is confident because he is genuinely capable—not arrogant or evil.
- Teammates react like tired adults dealing with an expensive operational problem, not cartoon victims.
- One visual joke per episode.

Avoid fights, blood, humiliation, crude locker-room humour, macho posturing, or jokes about safety and injury. The campaign should remain usable with professional-services buyers who may not care about hockey.

## Fiction and rights boundary

The broad territory is a fictional low-budget early-1980s underdog hockey comedy. It must not reproduce or closely imitate *Slap Shot* or another identifiable film.

Never use:

- existing film characters, names, dialogue, plot beats, hairstyles, costumes, team identities, or recognizable scenes;
- NHL, WHA, AHL, CHL, or real minor-league logos and uniforms;
- celebrity or athlete likenesses;
- identifiable arenas or real team marks;
- music written to resemble a recognizable film score or broadcast theme.

Use original characters, plain fictional uniforms, an unidentifiable community arena, and original audio. Public captions should identify scenes as fictional campaign material when that is not otherwise obvious.

## World bible

These are working production defaults. They may be changed before a series begins. Once generation starts, change a locked detail only by updating this brief and the reference pack first.

### Time and place

- **Year:** 1983.
- **Region:** fictional small Canadian prairie city; never name a real municipality.
- **League:** struggling adult semi-professional/minor-league circuit; never name a real league.
- **Season:** mid-winter.
- **Economic texture:** low budget, repaired equipment, sparse crowds, practical people keeping an unstable club alive.
- **Technology:** analog clocks, corded phones, paper rosters, cassette players, tube televisions, handwritten notes. No modern devices.

### Home arena

The home rink must remain recognizable across episodes:

- low ceiling and painted green-grey cinderblock;
- off-white boards with scuffs and no advertising;
- old red-brown goal frame and off-white netting;
- weak fluorescent and sodium lighting with a slight green cast;
- fogged or scratched rink glass;
- narrow wooden benches and sparse metal bleachers;
- a faded navy door leading to the dressing-room corridor;
- small analog scoreboard with no readable team names;
- no polished professional-arena architecture.

### Dressing room

- cream painted cinderblock with a green cast;
- plain wooden bench on three walls;
- metal hooks, taped sticks, old thermos, canvas equipment bags;
- one beige corded phone and cork tactics board;
- no readable posters, sponsors, or branded equipment;
- cluttered but not filthy, dangerous, or degrading.

## Character bible

### `AI` — the new star

**Role:** Human-bodied personification of AI capability. Deliberately faceless; never robotic and never treated as a literal human identity.

**Locked appearance:**

- male-coded adult athlete, approximately 29–32 in apparent age;
- tall, lean athletic build, roughly 185 cm / 6'1";
- long stride, broad shoulders, narrow waist, and naturally confident posture;
- light-to-medium olive skin visible only on the neck or hands;
- thick dark-brown wavy hair with early-1980s volume, medium length, identifiable only from the back;
- no canonical facial features are designed, generated, or included in the reference pack;
- performance is restrained; the joke is carried through action, timing, and the team's reaction rather than facial mugging.

**Face-visibility lock:**

- `AI`'s face must never be visible or readable in any still, video frame, reflection, photograph, poster, monitor, or other image within the scene;
- preferred framings are direct rear view and rear three-quarter view, using the `AI` nameplate as the main identity cue;
- a full side-body view is allowed only when his head turns away from camera, his helmet-and-head angle keeps the facial plane fully out of view, or the composition excludes his head;
- front view, front three-quarter view, over-the-shoulder shots that reveal his face, readable side profile, facial close-up, and reverse shot are prohibited;
- do not use blur, tracking noise, or shallow focus as the primary concealment method; compose the face out of the shot so every frame remains safe during motion;
- other characters may show their faces. The restriction belongs only to `AI`.

**Locked uniform:**

- warm-cream wool/polyester jersey;
- navy shoulder yoke and cuffs;
- two narrow waist and sleeve stripes: faded rust orange and muted golden tan;
- plain navy pants and cream socks with matching stripes;
- black skates, brown gloves, and a plain white period helmet in every on-ice shot;
- back nameplate reads exactly `AI`, the capital letter `A` followed by capital `I`—never `AL`, `A1`, `A|`, or a longer name;
- no front crest, sponsor, brand, or real team identifier.

**Performance behavior:**

- movement is unusually fluid and technically correct;
- receives information instantly and acts without hesitation;
- maintains full confidence until another person supplies the missing context;
- does not become stupid after the mistake is revealed;
- learns when coached; later episodes should show improvement rather than an endless reset.

**Never depict:** a visible face or facial profile; eyes, nose, or mouth; a face in reflection or on an in-world image; metallic skin; implants; glowing eyes; circuitry; data overlays; computer voice; monotone affect; superhuman strength; violence; villainy; or childlike innocence.

### `THE COACH` — operating responsibility

**Role:** The person who eventually turns capability into a functioning team.

**Locked appearance:**

- older man, approximately 62–68;
- compact, slightly stocky build; strong shoulders softened by age;
- light-to-medium weathered skin from years in cold arenas;
- short thinning salt-and-pepper hair, combed back without a dramatic period hairstyle;
- heavy grey eyebrows, deep-set pale eyes, broad nose, square jaw, and two days of grey stubble;
- no moustache, no glasses, no hat, and no resemblance to a known actor, athlete, or hockey-film character;
- worn navy wool work jacket over a cream button-front shirt, faded rust knit tie loosened at the collar, dark trousers, and battered black shoes;
- carries the same scarred clipboard and short orange grease pencil in every episode.

**Locked voice:**

- low gravelly baritone, rough from years in cold rinks;
- clipped prairie-Canadian cadence without a comic regional accent;
- speaks quietly enough that people lean in rather than performing for the room;
- dry, tired, and matter-of-fact; never theatrical, sentimental, or mean-spirited.

**Performance behavior:**

- paces in short deliberate steps with the clipboard held low at one side;
- watches before reacting, usually with a set jaw or one slow look;
- asks the contextual question everyone else skipped;
- never delivers a magical speech or humiliates AI;
- may mutter a blunt observation, but is never verbally abusive;
- provides the rule, owner, or game context that makes the talent useful;
- respects AI's talent even when frustrated by his mistakes;
- functions as operator, not saviour, buffoon, bully, or scold.

### `THE GOALIE` — consequence and memory

**Working appearance:**

- man, approximately 38–45;
- stocky build, light skin;
- plain white period mask with no graphic design;
- older brown leather pads and matching cream/navy jersey;
- tired eyes and contained reactions.

**Performance behavior:**

- usually understands the consequence first;
- reacts with stillness, a slow look, or one exhausted gesture;
- never becomes the repeated victim of injury or humiliation.

### `THE CAPTAIN` — experienced user

**Working appearance:**

- man, approximately 40–47;
- medium build, dark curly hair, neat dark moustache;
- same team uniform, visibly repaired gloves;
- optional small cream `C` may be composited in post, never generated as a real-team mark.

**Performance behavior:**

- knows the unwritten team context;
- initially resents the assumption that talent makes experience irrelevant;
- becomes AI's practical translator rather than rival;
- represents experienced employees whose knowledge should be supported, not extracted or discarded.

### Supporting team

Keep two or three recurring teammates rather than generating a new crowd every episode. Preserve age range, body types, facial hair, and jersey wear. Do not make every player a young athletic model. The club should feel like experienced adults keeping something difficult running.

## Visual bible

### Image texture

- Period-authentic early-1980s sports-comedy photography.
- Capture language: 16mm film or soft broadcast zoom lens, then transferred to worn consumer video.
- Muted cream, navy, rust, brown, and rink green.
- Soft edges, visible grain, mild gate weave, analog chroma bleed, intermittent tracking noise.
- Fluorescent highlights may clip; shadows should retain dirty green and brown rather than modern teal/orange grading.
- Imperfect framing, slow zooms, late pans, and occasional partial crops.

### What it must not become

- clean digital footage with a retro filter;
- synthwave, neon, VHS glitch art, or music-video nostalgia;
- glossy period fashion photography;
- a professional sports commercial;
- a replica of an existing movie;
- a modern office metaphor wearing hockey costumes.

### Camera behavior

- Rink action: shoulder-mounted or rink-level tripod, 35–70 mm equivalent, slightly late to follow action.
- Dressing room: handheld 28–40 mm equivalent, camera at seated eye height.
- Management office: locked tripod, 40–50 mm equivalent, awkward negative space.
- When `AI` is present, place the camera behind him or to his rear side. Block movement so he never turns through a face-revealing angle, including during entrances, reactions, and cutaways.
- Use the back nameplate, white helmet, silhouette, skating gait, and unusually precise movement—not a face—as `AI`'s recurring identity system.
- Avoid drone, gimbal, speed-ramp, bullet-time, impossible tracking, and ultra-shallow modern bokeh.

### Format treatment

- Generate and archive a clean 4:3 master for every narrative shot.
- For 9:16 social delivery, preserve the 4:3 frame inside the vertical canvas where possible; use bone/ink/orange copy fields above or below rather than destroying the ensemble composition with a severe crop.
- For 1:1 and 4:5 feeds, crop only after verifying that `AI`, the mistake, and the reaction remain legible and that reframing does not expose `AI`'s face.
- Add typography and the Dirtyworks.ai end card in post. Do not ask the image/video model to render campaign copy.

## Motion and audio bible

### Motion

- Playback should resemble 24 fps film transferred to analog video; mild interlacing is acceptable.
- Comedy depends on readable action and reaction, not fast cutting.
- Hold reaction shots 6–12 frames longer than feels comfortable.
- AI moves fluidly; the camera and team move imperfectly.
- Use tracking noise sparingly at scene openings, cuts, or end-card transitions—not continuously.

### Audio

- Mono or narrow stereo field.
- Arena HVAC hum, skate cuts, boards, stick taps, distant crowd, weak PA, dressing-room room tone.
- Dialogue is short, plain, and period-neutral.
- Original score only: small organ, dry drums, modest bass, or no score.
- No recognizable sports theme, film quotation, popular song, or soundalike.
- Captions are required whenever dialogue carries the joke.

### Dialogue behavior

AI speaks concisely and confidently. His lines are delivered while he faces away, from a rear or safely obscured side angle, or offscreen. Never cut to a face or lip-sync close-up to establish that he is speaking. Teammates speak like adults at work, not sitcom archetypes.

The Coach speaks in a low gravelly baritone with clipped phrasing. His bluntness should feel earned by experience, not hostile. When he says something funny, he delivers it as an observation rather than a punchline.

Example:

```text
CAPTAIN: Wrong net.

AI turns his shoulders and helmet slightly toward the net. His face remains hidden.

AI: The shot was optimal.

COACH: The direction wasn't.
```

Do not make every episode end in a quip. Silence and one look may carry the joke better.

## Episode structure

### Six-to-twelve-second execution

1. **Capability — 0–3 s:** AI performs something impressive.
2. **Missing context — 3–7 s:** reveal the simple operating mistake.
3. **Human consequence — 7–9 s:** one restrained reaction.
4. **Brand turn — 9–12 s:** hard cut to campaign line and Dirtyworks.ai.

### Fifteen-to-twenty-second execution

1. Situation and team problem.
2. AI's impressive intervention.
3. Reveal of the missing context.
4. Coach identifies one operating rule.
5. Campaign line and CTA.

## Initial episode slate

### 01 — The own goal

AI scores beautifully into his own team's net and celebrates. Stage the celebration from behind or rear three-quarter, with the `AI` nameplate visible and the helmet keeping his face hidden. The puck, matching uniforms, and teammate reactions must make the mistake visually unambiguous.

**End-card options:** `PERFECT EXECUTION. WRONG DIRECTION.` / `AI NEEDS A COACH.`

### 02 — The wrong playbook

AI recites and executes a complicated play perfectly. Stage him over the shoulder from behind, without revealing his profile. The captain slowly turns the binder around: it belongs to the opponent. Keep all binder text abstract; the ownership reveal can be supplied through a simple post-produced color tab.

**End-card options:** `HE KNEW EVERY PLAY. JUST NOT OURS.` / `APPROVED SOURCES MATTER.`

### 03 — The optimal shift

AI calculates the perfect individual shift length and refuses to leave the ice as the rest of the line changes. Track him from behind or the rear side; do not let his head rotate toward camera. The team collides operationally, not physically.

**End-card options:** `ONE PLAYER IS NOT AN OPERATING MODEL.`

### 04 — The equipment list

AI prepares every possible piece of equipment in exact rows, but nobody renewed the arena booking. Show him from behind among the flawless rows, or use only his hands and uniformed torso. The team stands outside a locked rink with flawless gear.

**End-card options:** `THE DETAILS NEED AN OWNER.`

### 05 — The coached play

The coach supplies one piece of game context. AI pauses, adjusts, and makes the correct pass to the captain. Block their exchange with the coach's face visible and `AI` seen from behind, then follow the play from `AI`'s rear side. This episode proves the campaign is about management, not AI failure.

**End-card options:** `CAPABILITY, OPERATED.` / `SOMEBODY STILL HAS TO RUN THE TEAM.`

## Generation and continuity workflow

### Step 1 — Build the reference pack

Before producing more episodes, create and approve:

- `AI` full-body silhouette anchors: direct back, left rear three-quarter, right rear three-quarter, and safe side-body with the head turned away;
- `AI` helmeted head and shoulders from the back, plus an unhelmeted back-of-hair reference for off-ice scenes—never generate or approve a face portrait;
- `AI` movement anchors: rear-view skating stride, standing posture, stick handling, and restrained celebration;
- exact jersey and stripe reference with a verified `AI` nameplate;
- coach, goalie, and captain neutral portrait/full-body anchors;
- arena wide shot, net close-up, dressing-room wide shot, and tactics-board close-up;
- palette and analog-texture reference sheet.

Use the approved anchors as image references for every subsequent generation. A written description alone is not sufficient for recurring identity. `AI` continuity comes from silhouette, proportions, hair seen from behind, helmet, uniform, nameplate, gait, and movement—not a hidden canonical face.

### Step 2 — Lock a continuity manifest

Record for every shot:

```text
episode_id:
shot_id:
date_generated:
reference_images:
character_ids:
wardrobe_state:
location_id:
time_of_day:
camera_position:
lens_behavior:
action_before:
action_after:
must_preserve:
known_errors:
```

### Step 3 — Generate anchors, then motion

- Establish the start frame as a still before generating video.
- Use that approved still as the video reference or first frame.
- When the ending geometry matters, also establish an end frame.
- Generate one action and one camera instruction per clip.
- Prefer multiple 3–5 second clips assembled in edit over one model-generated 20-second scene.
- Composite the `AI` nameplate in post if the model cannot keep the two letters exact through motion.

## Review checklist

Reject or repair a generated shot when any answer is no:

- Does `AI` read as a human-bodied personification without becoming an identifiable person?
- Is `AI`'s face completely absent, including profile, reflection, photograph, monitor image, and transitional motion frames?
- Does `AI` match the approved silhouette, proportions, back-of-hair or helmet, gait, movement, and uniform?
- Does the Coach match the approved older male face, stubble, hair, wardrobe, clipboard, voice, and restrained performance?
- If visible, does the nameplate read `AI`, not `AL` or `A1`?
- Can a viewer understand the impressive capability before the mistake?
- Is the missing context visually legible without an explanatory paragraph?
- Are the team and experienced people treated respectfully?
- Does the footage look genuinely period-authentic rather than filtered?
- Is the arena consistent with previous episodes?
- Are all marks, uniforms, dialogue, and music original?
- Does the episode support managed operation rather than “AI is bad”?
- Can the clip be captioned and described accessibly?

## Distribution and test boundary

Initial use:

- founder and Dirtyworks.ai social accounts;
- short paid test only after the normal paid-media gate is met;
- campaign landing page, Notes article, or embedded social proof-of-concept;
- no site-wide photography replacement during the test.

Measure separately:

- business-audience engagement versus hockey/nostalgia engagement;
- whether viewers can restate the operating message unaided;
- qualified profile visits, conversations, and campaign-assisted enquiries;
- comments that interpret the campaign as anti-AI, robot-related, or a movie imitation;
- production effort required to maintain character and period continuity.

Set the numeric success threshold against the account's observed content baseline before launch; do not choose a threshold after seeing results. Consider broader photography integration only after at least two creative cycles demonstrate both sustained audience response and correct message comprehension among target buyers.

## Accessibility and disclosure

- Caption all dialogue and meaningful arena audio.
- Do not rely on color alone to distinguish teams or explain the mistake.
- Write alt text that states the scene and the operating joke without claiming a real event.
- Where context requires, label the work `Fictional campaign scene` in the caption or accompanying page.
- Never present generated people as employees, customers, athletes, or participants in a real Dirtyworks.ai engagement.
